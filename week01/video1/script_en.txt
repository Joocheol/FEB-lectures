## [S03] The question of this episode (1:30)

Here is the question for this episode.
Banks sell complicated structured products with prospectuses dozens of pages long.
How can we understand such a product in a single picture?
The answer is surprisingly simple.
Strip away the name and the marketing, and just write down how much the product pays in the future.
The amount a financial product pays in the future is called its payoff.

## [S04] A real product: digital buffered notes (2:30)

Let us start with a real product.
In July twenty twenty-six, J.P. Morgan issued a product called digital buffered notes, linked to the S and P five hundred index.
The face amount is one thousand dollars per unit, and what you receive at maturity depends on the index level on that day.
Here are the terms.
As long as the index has not fallen more than fifteen percent below its initial level, the note pays a fixed return of seven point six nine percent.
That sounds complicated when you hear it in words.
So let us write it down state by state.

## [S05] Two states (4:00)

Call the initial index level S zero, and the index at maturity S T.
If the final index is at least eighty-five percent of the initial level, the investor receives one thousand seventy-six dollars and ninety cents.
Whether the index rose a lot, rose a little, stayed flat, or even fell within fifteen percent, makes no difference at all.
Within this range, the payment is always the same.
But if the final index is below eighty-five percent of the initial level, you start losing principal.
The further the index falls below the buffer, the less you receive.
So the world of this product splits into exactly two states.
One state where the payment is fixed, and one state where the payment follows the index down.
A structure where the payment depends on which future state is realized is called a state-by-state payoff.

## [S06] The payoff as a formula (6:00)

Let us translate what we just said into a formula, a piecewise function.
If the final index is at or above the buffer, you get one thousand seventy-six point nine dollars.
Below the buffer, you get the principal of one thousand dollars adjusted by the index decline.
You do not need to understand every term in this formula right now.
Only one thing matters.
Even a product with a prospectus dozens of pages long becomes this short, once you split the states and write down the payment in each one.

## [S07] Reading the picture (8:00)

Now let us look at the same thing as a picture.
The horizontal axis is the final index divided by the initial index, and the vertical axis is the payment at maturity.
To the right of the buffer at zero point eight five, the payment is flat at one thousand seventy-six point nine dollars.
To the left, the payment falls along a straight line as the index declines.
And that line is steeper than it looks.
If we normalize the vertical axis by the principal, the slope is not one, but one divided by zero point eight five, roughly one point one seven six.
Below the buffer, every additional one percentage point drop in the index costs the investor about one point one eight percentage points of return.
Now look closely at the buffer itself.
The moment the index reaches eighty-five, the payment jumps from one thousand dollars to one thousand seventy-six point nine, a jump of seventy-six dollars and ninety cents.
The open dot and the closed dot in the picture mark exactly this discontinuity.

## [S08] The scenario table (11:00)

Let us look at the scenario table that appears in the prospectus.
The two most striking rows are the index at eighty-five and at eighty-four point nine nine.
At eighty-five you receive one thousand seventy-six dollars and ninety cents, but just a tiny step down, at eighty-four point nine nine, you receive about nine hundred ninety-nine dollars and eighty-eight cents.
One hair below the boundary, and the entire fixed return disappears.
Now look at the other extreme.
Even if the index rises fifty percent, to one hundred fifty, you still receive only one thousand seventy-six dollars and ninety cents.
The upside is capped.
To summarize: this product absorbs a limited range of losses, and in exchange, it gives up the upside beyond a fixed return.
That one sentence is the summary of the entire prospectus.

## [S09] Quick recap (14:00)

Let us pause and recap.
The first step in understanding any financial product is to write down its payment in each future state.
We expressed the same information in three ways.
A table of states, a piecewise formula, and a picture.
They carry the same content, and you can start from whichever feels most natural.

## [S10] Demo: drawing payoffs in Python (15:00)

Now let us draw this picture ourselves.

When we plot it, we get exactly the same shape as the figure in the textbook.
Let us also check the table values.
Plug in eighty-five, and we get one thousand seventy-six point nine.
Plug in eighty-four point nine nine, and we get nine hundred ninety-nine point eight eight.

This is exactly what you will do in this week's lab session.

## [S11] Key takeaways (18:00)

Here are today's key takeaways.
A financial product can be expressed as a table, a vector, a function, or a picture of its state-by-state payoffs.
However complicated the name or the description, writing down the payment in each future situation makes the structure far simpler.
And the whole subject of financial engineering is this process: decompose complex products into simple payoffs, and reassemble their prices to price the original product.
